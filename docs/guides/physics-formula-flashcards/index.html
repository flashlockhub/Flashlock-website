How to learn physics formulas with flashcards and problems

By the FlashLock team · Published 16 November 2026 · Last reviewed 16 November 2026.

Quick answer: put the meaning, conditions and units of a physics formula on your cards, not just the equation. Then solve problems without being told which formula to use. Recall helps you start; choosing a model and checking the result require additional practice.

Start with the physical situation

Before making a card, describe what the equation models in plain language. What quantities are changing? What assumptions does the course make? A formula with no conditions attached is easy to apply in the wrong situation.

For example, v = u + at relates final velocity, initial velocity, acceleration and elapsed time for motion with constant acceleration. In a one-dimensional calculation, choose a positive direction and keep signs consistent. Do not silently use it when acceleration varies.

Make a small card set, not one overloaded card

  • Recall: what equation relates u, v, a and t for constant acceleration? Answer: v = u + at.
  • Meaning: what does a represent? Answer: the rate of change of velocity; here it is constant.
  • Conditions: when is this form appropriate? Answer: constant acceleration over the interval, with consistent direction and sign conventions.
  • Units: why can u and at be added? Answer: both have velocity units; m/s² multiplied by s gives m/s.
  • Rearrangement: solve for t when a is nonzero. Answer: t = (v − u)/a. If a is zero, this division is not valid.

Use the notation taught in your class; some texts write v₀ instead of u. Keep a source or section reference on the back so you can check a disputed condition.

Work a problem after recall

A cart moves at 2 m/s in the chosen positive direction and accelerates constantly at 3 m/s² for 4 s. Predict whether its final velocity should be greater or smaller before calculating. Then substitute: v = 2 + 3 × 4 = 14 m/s, in the positive direction.

Now close that example and change the question. What if acceleration points in the negative direction? What if you are given distance rather than time? The next step may require a different equation, not just changing the numbers in the same one.

Use three checks on every answer

  1. Units: do both sides have compatible dimensions? Convert units before substitution where needed.
  2. Direction and sign: does a negative value have a physical interpretation under your chosen convention?
  3. Limiting case: if time is zero or acceleration is zero, does the equation reduce to something sensible?

Dimensional consistency can expose an error, but it cannot prove that the model or numerical answer is correct. Compare with the situation and, when appropriate, a diagram or graph.

Turn mistakes into useful prompts

If you chose the wrong equation, make a card about the missing condition or clue. If you made an algebra error, practise the rearrangement on paper. If you knew the equation but could not begin, practise identifying known and unknown quantities across mixed problems.

Review a few cards on later days, then attempt problems with no formula heading. Do not judge readiness solely by how quickly you recognise an equation.
